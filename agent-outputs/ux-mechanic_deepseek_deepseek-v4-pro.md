**No — generic swipe cards would actively undermine learning goals here.**

Swipe-to-self-rate (“right = I know this”) is notoriously unreliable for language. It promotes a shallow familiarity judgment without retrieval, and users notoriously overestimate knowledge (Kornell & Bjork). For Egyptian Arabic, which demands audio‑script‑register triangulation on short phrases, a binary swipe reduces a rich cognitive task to mindless classification. Your persona — practicing before/after real conversations, not idle browsing — needs *production* in context, not passive recognition. The small screen already struggles to present RTL script, Franco transliteration, audio playback, and register nuance; overlaying a swipe gesture would crowd it further and encourage speed over accuracy. The app’s deliberate avoidance of streaks/hearts signals this isn’t about compulsive engagement loops; a swipe mechanic would pull against that ethos.

**A stronger, one-mechanic alternative: Speak‑First Scenario Cards.**

*Interaction:* A card presents a contextual prompt, e.g., “Your mother‑in‑law asks if you enjoyed the mahshi.” A prominent mic button invites the learner to *speak* the phrase aloud. The app records the attempt (no live ASR; it’s for self‑comparison). After releasing the mic, the card flips to reveal the correct phrase — written in Arabic script *and* Franco, with an audio model recording. The user then self‑evaluates using two large buttons: **“I nailed it”** (right‑swipe action) or **“Need more practice”** (left‑swipe). Right swiping marks the phrase for spaced review alongside explicit knowledge-of-result; left adds it to a daily drill queue.

*Why it fits:* This design situates the swipe *after* retrieval and production — the two strongest drivers of durable memory (the production effect, retrieval practice). It preserves contextual relevance, forces articulation of the sounds, and builds metacognitive accuracy because the user hears their own recording next to the model. The mechanic echoes real‑world use precisely: you speak first, then gauge success.

**Placement:** Introduce this as a new “Masri Drills” mode, accessible after completing a scenario‑based lesson. It would live between the four exercise types and the “say this tonight” mission — pulling from partner‑contributed phrases and register badges to generate personalized scenario prompts. It doesn’t replace any exercise; it’s the application layer that turns recognition into recall, directly preparing the learner for tonight’s real conversation.
