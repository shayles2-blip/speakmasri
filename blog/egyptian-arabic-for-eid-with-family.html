Egyptian Arabic for Your First Eid with the Family

You don't need a dozen phrases for your first Eid with an Egyptian family — you need the right six, said in the right order. Here they are.

TLDR

  • The one phrase everyone uses, to everyone, is كل سنة وانت طيب (kol sana w enta tayyeb) — the universal Eid greeting.
  • Greet the elders first, with a blessing, before anything else.
  • Compliment the food once you've eaten it, not before.
  • Asking for seconds politely is expected, not rude.

The one phrase you actually need

If you only learn one thing before your first Eid with an Egyptian family, make it this:

كل سنة وانت طيب
kol sana w enta tayyeb
"Eid greeting (to anyone)"

It works on everyone — elders, peers, kids, people you've just met five minutes ago. You genuinely cannot overuse it on Eid day.

These aren't random — they follow the day

Six phrases can feel like a list to memorize in no particular order. They're not. Said in the wrong sequence, even a correct phrase can land oddly; said in the right one, you look like you know exactly what you're doing.

Walk in and greet the elders first, before anyone else, with the blessing: الله يخليك لينا (Allah yekhalleek leena). This is a formal register on purpose — it's the one moment of the day where getting the respect right matters more than sounding casual and easy.

Once food is actually in front of you and you've eaten some, that's when the compliment lands: الأكل تحفة، تسلم إيدِك (el-akl tuhfa, teslam eedek). Said before you've taken a bite, it reads as empty flattery. Said after, it reads as genuine.

If you actually want more, ask — ممكن آخد زيادة شوية؟ (momken akhod zeyada shwayya?) isn't a phrase you save for when you're desperate, it's the normal, expected way to accept more food at an Egyptian table.

Somewhere in the middle of the gathering, a warm, general compliment to the room — كلكم شكلكم حلو أوي (kolkom sheklokom helw awi) — does a lot of social work for very little effort.

And when there's clearly something to be done — dishes, setting up, carrying things — offering costs you nothing and means a lot: أساعد في إيه؟ (asaed fi eh?).

Why this matters beyond one holiday

This is really a pattern that shows up far beyond Eid: learning Masri for real family moments isn't about accumulating vocabulary, it's about knowing which phrase belongs to which moment. The same six words said in a random order still communicate, technically — but said in the order the day actually unfolds, they show you've paid attention to how the family does things, not just to what the words mean.

The bottom line

You don't need to prepare for every possible Eid conversation. You need six phrases, used at the right moments: greet everyone, bless the elders first, compliment the food after you've eaten, ask for more without hesitating, compliment the room, and offer to help. That's a full, respectful Eid — not a performance, just attention to sequence.
