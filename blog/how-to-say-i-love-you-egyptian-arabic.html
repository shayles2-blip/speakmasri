How to Say "I Love You" in Egyptian Arabic (Not the Textbook Version)

The real, spoken way to say "I love you" in Egyptian Arabic, plus how it differs from the formal Arabic version most courses teach, and how Egyptians actually use it in a sentence.

TLDR

  • The natural, spoken way to say "I love you" in Egyptian Arabic (Masri) is بحبك, said as bahebak to a man or bahebik to a woman.
  • This is not the same as the Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) phrase you'll find in most general Arabic references, أحبك (uhibbuka / ahebbak) — formal, written-register Arabic, not something Egyptians actually say out loud to a partner.
  • حبيبي / حبيبتي (habibi / habibti, "darling" / "sweetheart") and إنت قلبي (enta 'alby, "you're my heart") are close companions to "I love you" in real conversation, worth knowing alongside it.
  • "Habibi" isn't just a standalone pet name — Egyptians use it inside full sentences constantly, e.g. asking a partner for a quick favor.
  • Getting the gendered ending right (-ak vs. -ik) is a small detail that makes a big difference in how natural you sound.

The real answer: بحبك (bahebak / bahebik)

If you search "how to say I love you in Egyptian Arabic," you'll find a lot of pages that just hand you the Modern Standard Arabic phrase and call it done. That's a mismatch, because MSA is the formal, written register of Arabic, used in news and books, not the language people actually speak to each other. General reference note, not from SpeakMasri's own course data: the commonly cited MSA phrase is أحبك (uhibbuka, or ahebbak in a more casual reading of it). You may see it in a phrasebook or a general Arabic-learning site. It's real Arabic — it's just not the Arabic your partner's family speaks at home.

The version Egyptians actually say is:

بحبك
bahebak (to a man) or bahebik (to a woman)
"I love you"

That single letter at the end, -ak versus -ik, is doing real work. It's not a typo or a dialect variant — it's Egyptian Arabic's grammatical gender agreement, and saying the wrong ending is one of the fastest ways to sound like you memorized a phrase instead of meaning it. Get comfortable switching between the two based on who you're actually talking to.

The words that travel with it

"I love you" rarely shows up alone in real conversation. A few phrases from Masri's intimate register tend to travel together with it:

ScriptTransliterationMeaning
حبيبي / حبيبتيhabibi / habibti"Darling" / "Sweetheart"
إنت قلبيenta 'alby"You're my heart"
بحبكbahebak / bahebik"I love you"

Habibi (to a man) and habibti (to a woman) are probably the single most recognizable Arabic words of affection worldwide, largely because of how often they show up in Egyptian music and film. But knowing the word isn't the same as knowing how it's actually used.

"Habibi" isn't just a pet name — it's a sentence starter

Here's where a lot of learners stop too early: they learn habibi as a standalone word and never see it inside a real sentence. Egyptians use it constantly as a natural way to address someone they're close to, mid-conversation, not just as a dramatic standalone declaration. For example:

يا حبيبي، ترجملي ده بسرعة.
Ya habibi, taragmli da b sora.
"Babe, translate this for me quick."

That's not a romantic-movie line — it's an ordinary, slightly rushed request you'd actually hear at home. This is the real texture of the intimate register: affectionate language woven into completely mundane moments, not reserved for big declarations. If "I love you" is the phrase you're building toward, sentences like this are what it sounds like once you're actually comfortable — not performing a memorized line, but talking normally with warmth built in.

Why this distinction matters beyond one phrase

This isn't just a quirk of one romantic phrase — it's the same MSA-vs-Masri gap that shapes everything about learning Arabic for a real relationship. MSA gives you a version of "I love you" that's technically correct and would read fine in a formal letter. Masri gives you the version your partner actually says, and actually wants to hear back. What Is Egyptian Arabic? covers why generic Arabic courses default to MSA and why that's the wrong starting point when your real goal is a specific person, not a pan-Arab formal standard.

And "I love you" is just one register out of several you'll need. A phrase that works with a partner (bahebak, habibi) would land very differently said to your father-in-law. The bottom line

If you want to say "I love you" in a way that actually sounds like Egyptian Arabic and not a phrasebook, it's بحبك — bahebak to a man or bahebik to a woman — not the MSA phrase most general references hand you. Pair it with habibi/habibti and enta 'alby, and start noticing how naturally those words fold into ordinary sentences, not just big romantic moments.
