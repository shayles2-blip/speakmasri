Egyptian Arabic for Taxis, Haggling, and Ordering Food

Not a phrasebook's idea of "travel Arabic" — the actual sentences that get you a fair taxi fare, a real price at a stall, and exactly what you wanted at a koshary counter.

TLDR

  • Three situations, one skill: naming a price, stopping a taxi where you want, and ordering food the way locals actually phrase it.
  • Haggling has a shape: state the price, push back, and close with a number, not a shrug.
  • Taxi Arabic is mostly about giving instructions, not asking permission.
  • Café and koshary orders use very specific words for "how much sugar" and "how much sauce" — get those two words right and you'll sound like you've done this before.

Naming a price: the shape of a haggle

A haggle in Egyptian Arabic isn't a fight, it's a short back-and-forth with a predictable rhythm: ask, object, push, close.

ScriptTransliterationMeaning
بكام ده؟bekam da?"How much is this?"
ده غالي أوي!da ghali awi!"That's too expensive!"
يلا بقى، اعملهولي بسعر حلوyalla ba'a, emholi beser helw"Come on, make it a good price"

That's the same yalla from everyday Masri, doing its usual job of nudging things along — here nudging a price down instead of nudging someone out the door.

ScriptTransliterationMeaning
عشرين جنيه وخلاصeshreen geneh w khalas"Twenty pounds, final"
معاك فكة؟maak fakka?"Do you have change?"

That fourth line does double duty — it's also where you'd swap in whatever number you're actually offering. If you've read what "khalas" means, this is exactly the "final" sense of it in action: a number followed by khalas closes the negotiation, not just the sentence.

Giving a taxi driver instructions, not asking permission

The taxi phrases below aren't questions you're hoping get answered politely — they're short, direct instructions, which is the normal register for this situation, not rudeness.

ScriptTransliterationMeaning
بكام لوسط البلد؟bekam le west el balad?"How much to downtown?"
شغّل العدّاد لو سمحتshaghal el addad law samaht"Turn on the meter, please"
على جنب هناala gamb hena"Stop here on the side"
خلي الباقي ليكkhali el ba'i leek"Keep the change"
دي مش الأجرة العاديةdi mesh el ogra el adeya"That's not the normal fare"

Notice "law samaht" ("please") softens the meter request without turning it into a question — you're still asking for something you're entitled to, just politely. That's a small register move worth copying elsewhere.

Ordering like you've done this before

Café and street-food orders in Egypt run on a handful of precise modifier words. Get the sugar and spice words right, and the rest takes care of itself.

ScriptTransliterationMeaning
شاي لو سمحتshay law samaht"A tea, please"
قهوة مظبوط'ahwa mazboot"Coffee, medium sugar"
سادةsada"Without sugar"
عصير مانجا فريشaseer manga fresh"A fresh mango juice"
يا أسطى!ya osta!"Waiter!" (getting attention)
واحد كشري وسطwahed koshari wasat"One medium koshary"
تقلية زيادةta'leya zeyada"Extra fried onions"
شطة خفيفshatta khafeef"Easy on the hot sauce"
تيك أوايtake away"Take away"
هنا جوهhena gowa"Eating in"
ترشحلي إيه؟torashahli eh?"What do you recommend?"
عايز دهayez da"I'd like this one"
من غير لحمة، أنا مباكلش لحمةmen gheir lahma, ana mabakolsh lahma"No meat, I'm vegetarian"

"Mazboot" (medium sugar) is worth memorizing on its own — it's the default most Egyptians order coffee at, and saying it correctly instantly signals you're not ordering off a menu translated for tourists.

What this guide isn't

This is street-and-café Arabic specifically — haggling, taxis, and ordering food — not a full airport-to-hotel travel phrasebook. If you're learning for a partner or their family rather than for getting around, Learn Egyptian Arabic for the People You Love covers that ground instead, and Egyptian Arabic Phrases by Register explains why the direct, no-frills tone above is completely normal here and would be too blunt in a formal or family setting.

The bottom line

Getting around Cairo in Masri isn't about a huge vocabulary — it's about a small set of exact phrases used with the right directness: name your price, give the driver clear instructions, and order with the specific words locals use. Get those down and you'll stop sounding like you're reading off a card.
